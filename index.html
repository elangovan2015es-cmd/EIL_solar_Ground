Pillar 2 — Low Project Cost & Fast Execution
Why does a Rs 10 Lakh per MW saving matter more than you think?
＋
Q. Why does keeping project cost low matter so much?
In a solar project, all revenue is fixed by the tariff — you cannot sell more units than the sun gives you, and you cannot charge more than what the customer agreed. The only lever you have full control over is what you spend to build it.
Every Rs 1 Crore saved in project cost reduces your debt by Rs 70 Lakhs (at 70% debt). That cuts your annual interest by Rs 6–7 Lakhs for 10 years. Over the project life, Rs 1 Crore saved at construction = Rs 60–80 Lakhs saved in debt service.
Rule of thumb: For a 20 MW project, a Rs 10 Lakh/MW saving = Rs 2 Crore total saving = Rs 1.4 Crore less debt = ~Rs 12 Lakh/year less interest payment = DSCR improves by 0.05–0.08×. That can be the difference between a bankable and non-bankable project.
Q. Why does project schedule matter for viability?
Every month of delay is a month of no revenue while interest meter is running. If your term loan disbursal starts in Month 1 but the plant commissions in Month 8 instead of Month 6, you pay 2 extra months of interest on the full loan amount — for a Rs 80 Crore loan at 9.5%, that is Rs 1.27 Crore wasted.
Beyond interest, delay means:
  • Customer's TNEB bill keeps running — eroding goodwill before even one unit is supplied
  • Your equity is locked up and not earning — opportunity cost for the promoter
  • If delay pushes commissioning past a TNERC tariff revision date, your Year 1 tariff could be lower than projected
  • Monsoon delay = first 3–4 months of low-generation summer lost = Year 1 PLF drops significantly
Target: Financial closure to commissioning in 9–12 months for a 20 MW ground mount. Key milestones: Land registration (M1), EPC contract (M2), equipment order (M3), civil works (M4–M6), module installation (M6–M8), TNERC inspection & metering (M9–M10), first injection (M10–M11).

Pillar 3 — Bankable Customers
Why 1–2 months invoice advance or equity stake is non-negotiable
＋
Q. What makes a customer "bankable"?
Your solar project's entire revenue depends on customers paying their monthly electricity invoice on time. If one large customer defaults for 3 months, your project may miss a loan EMI — and that triggers a bank penalty or NPA classification.
A bankable customer has:
  • Audited financials — can they demonstrate ability to pay Rs X Crore/year in electricity bills?
  • Credit history — no defaults with TNEB, vendors, or banks
  • Long-term business stability — is their industry cyclical? Can they survive a downturn and still pay?
  • Legal standing — GST-registered, ROC-compliant, no regulatory show-cause notices
Q. Why ask for 1–2 months invoice as advance or equity?
This serves two purposes simultaneously:
As Advance (Security Deposit)
Works like TNEB's own advance deposit. If the customer delays payment, you deduct from the advance. This gives you 1–2 months of runway before a payment crisis hits your EMI. It is standard practice in group captive contracts — most customers accept it because it mirrors TNEB's terms.
As Equity (Skin in the Game)
If the customer takes even 1–2% equity in the SPV, they become a co-owner, not just a buyer. Co-owners do not walk away from their own project. Their equity stake aligns their interest with yours for 25 years — and it counts toward the 26% collective equity compliance under TNERC Group Captive rules.
Practical example: A customer consuming 5 MW allocation has a monthly bill of ~Rs 45 Lakhs. Two months advance = Rs 90 Lakhs as security. Even if they default for 6 weeks, you are protected. Their 2% equity at Rs 1 Crore also gives them board representation alignment.
Q. What should the Power Sale Agreement (PSA) include to protect viability?
  • Floor price clause — selling price cannot fall below the break-even threshold shown in the Sensitivity page
  • Minimum offtake obligation — customer must consume at least 80% of their allocated units or pay for them anyway
  • Escalation clause — price escalates at CPI or TNEB tariff revision, whichever is higher
  • Exit penalty — minimum 12 months notice with financial penalty equal to 6 months billing if they exit early
  • Dispute resolution — arbitration clause to avoid court delays

Pillar 1 — Debt: The Silent Risk
Like stress in health — hard to define, but too much kills the project
＋
🏥 Think of Your Project Like a Human Body
A solar project has measurable vital signs — just like your annual health checkup. Most parameters have a normal range, a warning range, and a danger zone. Debt is like stress — unlike blood pressure or cholesterol, there is no single number that defines "too much." But everyone who has lived with excessive stress knows what it does over time. So does a project with excessive debt.
Debt ↔ Stress
Difficult to define a safe limit. Too much is clearly bad. It compounds silently — small shocks become crises when debt is high. Keep it low.
DSCR ↔ Blood Pressure
Measurable, trackable, with a clear danger threshold (1.20×). Like BP 120/80 is normal, DSCR 1.40× is healthy. Below 1.20× — the system is under strain.
CUF / PLF ↔ Cholesterol
Higher is generally better for the project (more generation = more revenue), but must be realistic. Overstating CUF in projections is like ignoring a cholesterol report.
Collections ↔ Blood Sugar
Cash coming in on time = healthy glucose levels. Delayed payments = elevated sugar. Chronic delay (diabetes) = project cannot function normally without intervention.
O&M Cost ↔ Weight
Slightly above target is manageable. Significantly overweight (O&M running 30% above budget) signals equipment failure or poor maintenance discipline — fix it before it becomes critical.
Imponderables ↔ Sudden Illness
Macro events — RBI rate cycles, TNERC policy reversals, a global supply chain shock, or a once-in-a-decade weather event. Science cannot predict these. Build resilience, not dependence on perfect conditions.
Q. How much debt is safe for a Group Captive solar project?
The industry standard is 70:30 (70% debt, 30% equity). Banks push for higher equity; developers push for lower. The right answer depends on your DSCR headroom — not a fixed percentage.
Debt Level Typical DSCR Impact Verdict
Below 50%DSCR ≥ 1.60×✔ Excellent. Very comfortable. Project can absorb shocks.
50% – 65%DSCR 1.40–1.60×✔ Good. Standard for well-structured projects.
65% – 75%DSCR 1.25–1.40×⚠ Acceptable. Watch closely. Limited cushion.
75% – 80%DSCR 1.20–1.28×⚠ Danger zone. Any adverse event breaks the DSCR floor.
Above 80%DSCR < 1.20×✘ Non-bankable. No responsible lender should approve this.

Rule of Thumb: Target debt at or below 65% of total project cost. The 5–15% extra equity you put in buys you enormous resilience — it is the difference between a project that survives a bad monsoon year and one that goes into lender workout proceedings.
Q. What about personal guarantees and corporate guarantees — how dangerous are they?
This is one of the most underappreciated risks in project finance. When you sign a personal guarantee for a project loan, you are no longer just an equity investor — you are the lender's insurance policy. If the project defaults, the bank comes after your personal assets.
Personal Guarantee Risk
  • Bank can attach personal property — home, investments, savings
  • Applies even if the project underperforms due to external factors (TNERC order, bad monsoon)
  • Continues until loan is fully repaid — typically 10–12 years
  • Cannot be withdrawn unilaterally once signed
Corporate Guarantee Risk
  • The listed parent company becomes contingent liable for the SPV loan
  • Shows up in your balance sheet as a contingent liability — affects credit rating
  • If SPV defaults, the guarantee is invoked — impacts the parent company's borrowing capacity
  • SEBI and stock exchange disclosure obligations triggered
Caution: Never give a personal or corporate guarantee unless the project's DSCR is comfortably above 1.40× across all stress scenarios (see ). The guarantee is not just paperwork — it is a mortgage on your future. Negotiate hard to limit it: offer a pledge of project assets and cash flows instead, and cap the guarantee at 1× the outstanding loan balance.
Q. What about risks that no model can predict — the imponderables?
Science and financial modelling can calculate DSCR, break-even thresholds, and return multiples with precision. What they cannot predict are the macro shocks — the events that change the rules of the game entirely.
Imponderable Risk Why It Cannot Be Modelled How to Build Resilience
RBI interest rate cycleDepends on global inflation, geopolitics, US Fed decisionsFix your loan rate at financial closure. Never leave it floating.
TNERC policy reversalRegulatory changes can eliminate OA concession, add new levies, or restrict group captive rulesSign 25-year PSAs. Lobby through ISMA/industry bodies. Diversify customer categories.
Climate extremes10-year cloud cover anomalies, cyclones, dust storms — no model predicts with certaintyUse P90 irradiation data (not P50) for conservative projections. Keep 3-month O&M reserve fund.
Industrial slowdownRecession hits your customers — they reduce shifts, consume less, ask to exit PSAsCustomer mix diversification (Pillar 3). Exit penalty clauses in PSA. IEX as backup offtake.
Technology disruptionBattery storage becomes cheap enough that customers prefer own rooftop — your PSA customers may not renewBuild 25-year PSAs with renewal options. Stay ahead — offer battery storage add-ons yourself.
Module degradation surpriseManufacturer warranty claims are valid but enforcement is difficult if company exits IndiaBuy from Tier 1 manufacturers with Indian presence. Insist on bankable warranties. Get module insurance.
The right mindset: You cannot eliminate imponderable risk. You can only build a project so robust that it survives even when two or three imponderables happen simultaneously. Low debt, diversified customers, fixed interest rate, and strong PSA contracts — these four together give your project the immune system it needs to survive what the model cannot predict.

Pillar 4 — Right Mix of Customers
Industrial, Commercial, Exchange — why diversification protects your revenue
＋
Q. Why not just find one large industrial customer and sign a full 20 MW PSA?
It is tempting — simpler to manage, one contract, one payment. But it creates a single-point-of-failure risk. If that one customer shuts a plant, restructures, or goes bankrupt, your entire 20 MW revenue stream collapses overnight. Your EMI does not stop. Your O&M does not stop. Only their payment stops.
A well-structured portfolio protects against this. Even TNEB — the largest distribution company in Tamil Nadu — does not rely on one industrial consumer for 100% of its revenue.
Q. What is the ideal customer mix for a 20 MW project?
Customer Type Suggested % of Allocation Why Include Risk to Watch
Large Industrial (HT-I)40–50%Highest bill, largest saving, easiest meteringCyclical industry shutdowns, shift to captive generation
Commercial (HT-III)25–35%Higher tariff (Rs 9.40/kWh) = larger savings = stickier customersLower consumption — need multiple smaller accounts
Exchange / Short-term PPA10–15%Fills capacity during customer shutdowns, IEX gives transparent priceIEX price volatility — do not rely on this as base revenue
Medium Industrial (HT-II)10–15%Stable 3-shift operations, lower default riskSmaller allocation per customer — more contracts to manage
Q. What is the IEX / Exchange route and when should it be used?
The Indian Energy Exchange (IEX) allows you to sell surplus generation in the day-ahead market at real-time prices. This is a safety valve — not a primary revenue source.
Use it when: A customer goes offline for a planned shutdown (annual maintenance, festive holidays, market slowdown). Instead of wasting generation, sell it on IEX at the prevailing DAM rate.
Do not rely on it: IEX prices are volatile — they can fall to Rs 2–3/kWh in high-generation months (March–May when all solar projects peak together). Your PSA price is typically Rs 6.50–7.50/kWh. The difference is significant.
Key insight: Design your PSA portfolio to cover 85–90% of generation. The remaining 10–15% can go to exchange as upside. If you design for 100% PSA coverage, a single customer shutdown creates a crisis. If you design for 85%, the same event is just a minor variance.

Pillar 5 — Actual vs Projected Monitoring
Constant comparison: generation, cash flow, deviations — and acting on them
＋
Q. Why is monitoring actual vs projected so critical?
Your financial model (built in this app) is a projection based on assumptions — assumed PLF, assumed tariff, assumed O&M cost, assumed customer payments. Reality will always differ. The question is: by how much, and in which direction?
A project that hits 19% CUF instead of projected 21.3% loses ~10% of revenue every year. Over 25 years, that is the difference between 9× return multiple and 6× return multiple. But if you catch the deviation in Month 3 and trace it to soiling or inverter underperformance, you can fix it. If you notice it in Year 3, you have lost Rs 2–3 Crore unnecessarily.
Q. What should be tracked monthly?
Metric What to Compare If Deviation Exceeds 5% — Do This
Generation (MU)Actual kWh vs projected kWh for that monthCheck inverter logs, soiling level, shading, PR ratio
PLF / CUF %Actual CUF vs projected CUF (from this model)Compare with irradiation data (SOLARGIS/NASA). If irradiation is normal but CUF is low — equipment issue
Net Revenue (Rs Cr)Actual invoices raised vs projected revenueCheck if any customer disputed units, if metering is correct, if OA billing matches TANGEDCO statement
CollectionsInvoices raised vs cash actually receivedAny customer overdue beyond 30 days — invoke advance deposit. Overdue beyond 60 days — legal notice
O&M CostActual spend vs projected O&M budgetIf overspending — identify if it is preventive or corrective maintenance. Corrective = equipment issue to fix
DSCR (trailing 12m)Actual EBITDA ÷ Actual debt serviceIf below 1.30×, alert your lender proactively. Do not let them discover it first
Q. What are the early warning signs that a project is drifting into trouble?
  • CUF drops >2% below projection for 2 consecutive months — check Performance Ratio (PR). Normal PR: 75–82%. Below 72% = serious equipment or soiling issue.
  • One customer delays payment twice — do not wait for third default. Send formal notice and invoke advance.
  • O&M cost exceeds budget by 15%+ — usually signals a warranty claim situation. Escalate to EPC contractor immediately.
  • TANGEDCO wheeling statement shows different units than SCADA — metering dispute. Resolve before it compounds into a billing problem.
  • IEX prices crash below Rs 3/kWh for 3+ months — do not rely on exchange. Accelerate finding a PSA customer for that unallocated capacity.
Q. This app has an "Actual vs Projected" page — how does it help?
The Actual vs Projected page (in the sidebar) lets you enter your real monthly generation, revenue, and payments and compare them against this model's projections. It computes variance automatically and flags deviations in red.
Use it every month on the 5th — once all TANGEDCO statements and customer payments are in. It takes 10 minutes to update and gives you a complete picture of where the project stands vs where it should be. That 10-minute discipline over 25 years is what separates a 9× return project from a 5× one.

TNERC Compliance
Monthly tracking & annual TNPDCL certificate
Annual obligation: At the end of each year submit to TNPDCL that Group Captive CPP status is maintained. Three rules must be met: (1) All customers collectively hold ≥26% SPV equity. (2) Total captive consumption ≥51% of net generation. (3) Each customer consumes proportionally to their equity stake (±10% tolerance per TNERC). This section tracks all three month by month.
SPV and plant details for annual certificate
Rule 1 — Collective equity ≥26% (CERC Captive Rules 2001)
The 26% rule applies to the total of all captive customers combined. Individual customers may hold any percentage. The developer/promoter holds the remaining equity. If total customer equity falls below 26%, CSS is reinstated retroactively for the entire year.
Rule 2 & 3 — Monthly generation and consumption tracking
Enter actual monthly generation (MU) from SLDC meter. Customer-wise allotment auto-calculates from equity proportion. Enter each customer's actual consumption. Variance and compliance status shown instantly.

🌥 C1. Tornado — Which Variable Hurts Most? (FCF Impact ±10%)
🌪 C1. What is the Tornado Chart?
What it shows: Each horizontal bar shows how much Year 1 Free Cash Flow (FCF) changes if that variable moves by ±10% from its current value. Green bar (right) = FCF improves when variable goes up 10%. Red bar (left) = FCF falls when variable goes down 10%.
Longest bar = biggest risk. The variable with the longest bar affects your cash flow the most. Focus your negotiations and risk management on the top 2–3 longest bars.
Example: If Selling Price has the longest bar, a 10% drop in your selling price hurts you more than a 10% rise in interest rate. That means your PSA floor price clause is more important than fixing the loan rate.
All values in Rs Crore (Year 1 FCF change). The chart updates live every time you move a slider above.
Horizontal bars show how much FCF changes when that variable is +10% (green) or −10% (red) from base. Longest bar = most sensitive variable. Values in Rs Crores.
📊 How to Read the Break-Even Table
Section C — Viability Threshold Analysis
The danger line. Each row shows the exact point at which your project DSCR falls to 1.20× — the minimum your bank requires. Cross it and the loan gets rejected.
What is DSCR?
DSCR = Operating Profit (EBITDA) ÷ Annual Loan Repayment. If DSCR = 1.50×, you earn Rs 1.50 for every Rs 1.00 you owe the bank. Banks require at least 1.20×. Year 2 is used — the first full repayment year after the moratorium ends.

💵 D. Selling Price vs FCF & DSCR
📊 D. What is the Selling Price vs FCF Chart?
What it shows: Each bar shows your Free Cash Flow (FCF) in Year 1 at a different selling price — from Rs 5.50 to Rs 8.50 in Rs 0.50 steps. The orange bar is your current selling price. The line shows DSCR (right axis).
How to read it: Bars below zero = the project loses money at that price. The red dashed line at DSCR 1.20× shows the lender minimum — any bar whose line point is below that is non-bankable.
What to do: Find the lowest price where FCF stays positive and DSCR stays above 1.20×. That is your minimum viable selling price. Do not sign a PSA below that price.
Each bar = FCF Yr-1 at that selling price. Orange bar = your current scenario. Line (right axis) = DSCR Yr-2. Red dashed = bank minimum DSCR 1.25×.
⚡ E. Injection Voltage Comparison — 110kV vs 33kV vs 11kV
⚡ E. What is the Voltage Comparison Chart?
What it shows: A side-by-side bar chart comparing Year 1 FCF and DSCR at three different grid injection voltages — 110 kV, 33 kV, and 11 kV. All other inputs stay the same.
Why voltage matters: Higher injection voltage = lower transmission loss. At 110 kV, TNERC T.O.7/2022 allows zero transmission loss charge. At 11 kV, losses can be 8–12%, cutting your revenue significantly.
TNERC rule: 20 MW and above must inject at 110 kV via a dedicated substation bay. Below 20 MW can use 33 kV. 11 kV is only for small projects — very high losses.
What to do: The tallest bar shows your best voltage option. If 110 kV shows significantly better FCF, invest in a dedicated bay — it pays back quickly through lower losses over 25 years.
Selling price is the same in all three cases. Only TNERC deemed transmission losses differ. Green bars show the advantage of 110kV EHT injection. Every rupee lost to line losses is permanent — no recovery possible.
📊 F. Sensitivity Matrix — Loss% × Interest Rate → FCF & EBITDA
📊 F. What is the FCF Sensitivity Matrix?
What it shows: A grid where each row = a different technical loss % (e.g. 4%, 6%, 8%…) and each column = a different interest rate % (e.g. 7%, 8%, 9%…). Each cell shows Year 1 FCF and EBITDA at that combination.
How to read it: Green cells = positive FCF (project earns money). Red cells = negative FCF (project loses money). Find where your current loss % row meets your current interest rate column — that is your base case cell.
What to do: Check how many rows and columns away from your base case turn red. If the cell immediately to the right (higher rate) or below (higher loss) turns red, your project has very little cushion. Negotiate a fixed interest rate and invest in loss reduction.
🟩 Green = better | 🟡 Amber = within 10% worse | 🔴 Red = materially worse than base. Orange border = base case. Values in Rs Cr.
💵 G. Net Realisation — Selling Price × OA Charge
💰 G. What is the Net Realisation Matrix?
What it shows: A grid where each row = a selling price (Rs/kWh) and each column = an OA charge (Rs/kWh). Each cell shows Net Realisation = Selling Price minus OA Charge — the actual Rs per unit the developer keeps.
Why it matters: The developer bears the OA charge — not the customer. So if OA rises (e.g. TNERC removes the 30% solar concession), your net realisation drops even though the selling price stays the same. This matrix shows exactly how much you keep at every combination.
How to read it: Green cells = healthy net realisation (>Rs 5/kWh). Red cells = dangerously low. Find your current SP row and OA column — that cell is your current net realisation. Check how many steps in any direction before it turns red.
Minimum viable: Net realisation must be high enough to cover O&M, insurance, and debt service. Any cell below Rs 4/kWh is a warning — check DSCR immediately.
Net realisation (Rs/kWh) = Selling Price − OA. This is the developer's revenue per unit. ≥ Rs 5.50 = good | Rs 4.50–5.50 = acceptable | < Rs 4.50 = borderline. Base case cell highlighted.
🏦 H. DSCR Matrix — Debt Ratio% × Interest Rate%
🏦 H. What is the DSCR Matrix?
What it shows: A grid where each row = a debt fraction % (how much of capex is borrowed) and each column = an interest rate %. Each cell shows the Year 2 DSCR — the first full repayment year.
Colour coding: Green ≥ 1.50× = strong, lender is comfortable. Amber 1.20–1.49× = acceptable, bank will lend. Red < 1.20× = non-bankable — bank rejects loan.
How to use it: Find your current debt % row and interest rate column. That cell is your project's current DSCR. The cells around it show what happens if the bank charges a higher rate or requires less debt. Take this matrix to your bank meeting — it demonstrates the project is robust across a range of lending conditions.
Negotiating with the bank: If the banker says they want 75% debt at 10.5%, find that cell. If it is green, the project is fine. If amber, you can still proceed. If red, you must either reduce the debt amount or negotiate a lower rate.
🟢 ≥ 2.0× excellent  🟢 ≥ 1.50× strong  🟡 1.20–1.49× acceptable (bank minimum)  🔴 < 1.20× non-bankable. Orange = base case.
🎯 I. 25-Year Return Multiple — PLF% × Total Loss%
🎯 I. What is the 25-Year Return Multiple Table?
What it shows: A grid where each row = a PLF/CUF % and each column = a total technical loss %. Each cell shows the Return Multiple — total 25-year cumulative FCF divided by equity invested. A multiple of 8× means Rs 8 back for every Rs 1 invested over 25 years.
Why 25 years: Solar projects have a 25-year PPA or design life. The return multiple tells the equity investor (the promoter) how many times they get their money back over the full project life — including the final salvage value.
How to read it: Green cells ≥ 8× = excellent return. Amber 5–8× = good. Red < 5× = marginal — reconsider the project structure. Find where your current PLF row meets your current loss % column.
Simple example: If your equity is Rs 10 Crore and the cell shows 9×, you get back Rs 90 Crore over 25 years = Rs 80 Crore profit. A bad monsoon region (low PLF) or high losses can bring this down to 5× or below.
Return Multiple = Total 25-yr FCF ÷ Equity invested. ≥ 10× excellent. Equity invested = Rs — Cr.
